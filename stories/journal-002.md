The first hint of daylight slipped through the gaps in the wooden shutters of Nox's room. He was already awake, sitting at the edge of the low cot, hands braced on his knees. His obsidian scales caught the thin morning light, dull black with a faint sheen like wet stone. His breath steamed in the cold, dry air, rising and vanishing. He rolled his kneck until it popped. 

The tick of the wall clock, the distant cluck of birds. Nothing else made a sound.

Nox pushed himself up, joints clicking. His tail dragged behind him as he moved to the basin and splashed water on his face, rubbing his eyes with a thick-fingered hand. The farmhouse was plain, floorboards cool and worn smooth by years of use. He pulled on his worn work trousers and a canvas shirt, pulling the suspenders over his broad shoulders. His boots were by the door; he shoved his feet in, wiggling his claws until the fit was right. A battered hat waited on a peg. He took it down and set it between his horns.

He stepped outside. The morning was cold and grey. The fields stretched out in ordered rows, stubbled earth patched with frost. The barn loomed across the packed yard, doors shut. The fence ran off in a zigzag, sagging in one spot where a post had rotted. Beyond the fence, the wild woods pressed close, dark and tangled.

Nox drew a slow breath. Dry grass, wood smoke from the chimney, the oily scent of boghogs in their pen. The air was damp, heavy with the promise of rain.

He crossed the yard, boots leaving deep prints. The barn doors gave a low groan as he pulled them open. Inside, the tindersows rustled in their pen. Three of them, the largest as big as a wheelbarrow. Their eyes glinted yellow in the dimness. They got fussy when cold; belching little sparks if anxious.

Nox grabbed a bucket and measured out scoops of grain. The tindersows pressed close, snuffling and bumping their wide snouts against the slats. One let out a crackling grunt that sent a puff of warm cinders from its nose. Nox opened the pen, set the feed troughs in place, and scattered the grain. The sows jostled for space, squealing, tails flicking. He checked the water barrel and broke the film of ice with a thick stick.

After a moment, he squatted next to the pen, watching the sows eat. Their thick hides shimmered with an undercurrent of heat. He reached out, scratching one between the ears. Its skin was warm—almost hot to the touch. The sow’s eyelids drooped, satisfied.

He stood and moved to the next stall, where the boghogs lay curled in a muddy heap. They looked like enormous boars, but with dark green pelts and wide, paddle-like snouts. One lifted its head, blinking sleepily. Bogs were simple creatures. Nox filled a slop trough with vegetable peelings and tough roots, then checked the hinges on their gate, tightening one with a spanner from his belt.

When the boghogs finished eating, Nox ran his hand down their bristly backs, checking for ticks or sores. He pulled a burr from one’s leg, tossing it into a tin pail, then moved to the far wall and cracked the window to let out the stale air.

Nox walked to the shed, ducking inside. It was cramped, shelves lined with jars of nails, tins of grease, coils of rope, and hooks lined with tools. In one corner stood his wood golem, slumped in its storage rack. The golem was six feet tall, humanoid, made of interlocked planks, whorled driftwood, and pegs for joints. A silver rune was burned into the chestplate, still faintly glowing. Nox circled the golem, checking each limb, running his hand over the joints, feeling for roughness or slack. One elbow stuck, so he took a cloth, wiped out the old sap, and worked a dab of oil into the gears until it moved freely. He checked the rune with a short muttered word; it flared, then faded.

He grabed and flicked the core nearbye. The golem's head jerked up, glass eyes flickering. It straightened, creaking, and looked at him.

"Haybales," Nox grunted.

The golem nodded once and trundled past him, ducking under the low doorframe. Nox followed it out, watching as the golem stomped to the stack by the barn and hefted a heavy bale with both hands. The golem’s arms flexed with a sound like bending willow. Nox adjusted the gate so the golem could pass, then watched it move bale after bale to the far paddock. He kept an eye on its gait, listening for any stutter or catch. The maintenance had taken; it moved smoothly.

With the animals seen to and the golem working, Nox grabbed a pair of gloves and made for the fields. He moved along the rows of winter barley, kneeling to check the dark soil. Frost lingered in the shaded spots. He pressed his thumb into the dirt, feeling for moisture. Just damp enough, but not soaked. He nudged aside a sprout, checking the pale roots for rot or signs of beetle infestation. Nothing yet.

At the far end of the field, weeds had begun to crowd the edges. Nox stooped and yanked them up one by one, stacking them in a burlap sack. He worked silently, breathing steady, shoulders moving beneath his shirt. The soil stuck to his claws. After a while, he straightened, wiped his hands on his trousers, and scanned the field. No sign of blight. Good.

Hours blended together into a day of honest labor. Vegetable beds-rows of winter cabbage, squat and pale green, thick leaves. Wind hissed through hedge gaps. Fingers numbed from the chill seeping into the gloves. Carrot beds needed thinning. A crow called from the woodlot. Nox dropped produce after produce into a pail, setting tops aside for the boghogs. Nox adjusted his hat, tucked it lower between his horns. A rhythm developed. Squat, pull, cut, throw. Heave, carry, deposit. Over and over and over until he blinked and realized just how much time had passed based on the amount of sweat pooling on his back.

Nox stood still, hands on hips, drawing in the air through his nose. The smell was sharper now, wind shifting, bringing the scent of damp leaves and the coming rain. The sky was darker, clouds heavy with water, the sun just a pale smear behind them.

He walked back to the barn, set the tools in their places, and wiped his hands on a rag. He checked the feed bins, making a note to refill them in the afternoon. A quick count of the stored grain, the water barrels, the sacks of root vegetables for the animals. Everything in order.

Nox sat on the stoop outside the barn, boots planted wide, arms resting on his knees. He watched the fields, the way the breeze rattled the dry stalks, the animals’ slow movements. His hands were rough, scales chipped here and there from years of labor. He flexed his fingers, checking for new aches.

A single drop of rain splashed against the back of his hand, followed by another, then a slow steady drizzle. Then rain fell harder, pattering against the barn roof, slicking dirt into mud, wind sharp and biting. Nox made one last round. Animals settled, gates latched, tools away, golem tucked. At the edge of the fields, he stood a moment longer. Rain washed over him, running in rivulets down the scales of his arms. The air was cold, but clean. He turned up the collar of his coat and looked out at his land: the dark, rich soil, the neat lines of crop and fence, the animals safe, the work done for now.

The fire burned low, a steady glow of coals pulsing behind the stove's iron grate. The room was warm, shadows moving softly along the wooden walls. Nox stretched out on the old couch, feet dangling off the opposite edge, one arm slung across his broad chest. Porthanos was seated at the head, book propped open in front of him, golden tail curled loosely up and around Nox's back. Nox's head rested in Porthanos' lap, black scales a deep contrast against the other's gold.

Porthanos read quietly, eyes moving over the pages. One hand cradled the book, the other absent-mindedly stroking the curve of Nox's brow ridge. Nox drifted, half-awake, the weight of the day fading behind the gentle touch and steady warmth. The house was silent but for the occasional crack from the fire, the turning of a page, and the cascading rain outside.

The world outside was black. Now and then, wind pressed against the window to make the glass creak, but it was distant. Nox listened to the fire and the breathing of his husband. He let himself sink into the comfort of small familiar noises: the clock ticking on the mantle, the kettle cooling in the kitchen, Porhtanos' tail occasionally tapping the floor.

Finally, Porthanos spoke, voice even. "They're talking again, in town," he said, running his thumb along Nox's jaw. "More than usual. Folk say the Saintillian border patrols are getting closer. Anaa at the mill says she saw three men in new boots walking the old river path. Didn't know them."

Nox kept his eyes close. "Could be traders. Plenty of strangers at the edge this time of year."

"Could be. But Marten's worried. He says someone's been buying up canvas and salt by the wagon, asking after the old bridge." He gave a small laugh. "Folk will make anything out to be trouble if they want it bad enough, but it feels different."

Nox grunted, shifting further into the comforting warmth. He tried to envision the Saintillac banners. Sharp clean edges of white-gold, rows and rows of spears filling out the muddy roads coming around the bend. Trying to conjure what that meant for the town was unreal. "Folk get nervous before planting," he said finally. "Nothing stires people up like what they don't know."

"Still," Porthanos said, "I'd rather know what's out there than wonder." His hand moved to Nox's horn, fingers tracing it. "Council's thinking of sending a pair to each outlying farm to check in. Marten's worried about you. Says you keep too much to yourself."

A swirl of color danced behind Nox's eyelids, as the hush of the evening tugged memories out from the floorboards of his mind. Running, pitter patter of feet, shrieks of joy. There had been six of them in total, a chaotic array of different scale color. Ma and Fa crowded them all into this very house, every room packed with beds and shoes and dishes and laughter. The kitchen table was too small for them all, so they ate in shifts, elbows bumping, bread passed back and forth. Noise on noise on noise. Someone always awake, or complaining, or laughing. Nights like this the embers could die down but they certainly wouldn't. The scratch of dice on wood, someone tuning an old lute they didn't know how to play, another child reading out from a book. Ma's voice would cut through it all to tell them to go to bed. Other times they would crowd this very couch, Saba curling up in his lap, while Da read to them from that book of legends. The one about the three blind mice had always been her favorite.

> Three blind mice—one made of bone, one of gold, and one of ash—ascended the Thorned Hill to slay the Serpent of the Sky. The serpent, slick with stars and smoke, could not be killed by tooth or claw. So the mice sang a lullaby so pure, it buried the beast in a cradle of dawnlight. They left the cradle at the hill's peak, warning all: “Do not wake the weapon that sleeps in flame.”

A bittersweet ache settled low in his chest. He wondered what his siblings would think of the farm now. The new fence posts, the vegetable beds, the boghog pen in place of the orchard. Would they recognize it as home, or just pass it by cause it was unfamiliar?

"What is it?" Porthanos asked.

Nox shook his head. He kept his eyes on the fire, watched the last log settle and split. "Just thinking about when it was busier here. When the house was full."

They sat in silence for a moment. Nox didn't shy awake from the ache in his chest. Ma's voice rang in his head, *grief is just love trying to persevere.*

After a time, Pothanos spoke again. "We make a good home here, you and me."

Nox nodded, throat tight. He reached up, covering Porthanos’ hand with his own, their claws fitting together easily. “We do,” he said, and meant it.

Porthanos picked up the book again, thumbing through the pages. “Want me to read for a bit?” he asked.

Nox nodded, letting his eyes close again. “Yeah. I’d like that.”

So Porthanos read, voice low and measured, the words flowing through the room. Nox didn’t care much about the story, something about a long-ago war, a hero who came home changed, but he listened for the sound, the cadence, the way it filled the house around them.
